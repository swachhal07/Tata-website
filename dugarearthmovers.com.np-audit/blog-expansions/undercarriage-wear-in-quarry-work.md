BODY STARTS HERE

Undercarriage is the single largest running cost on a tracked excavator in quarry work, and most contractors treat it as weather — something that happens to you.

It is not. When we measure worn components against the operating hours on the machine, the spread between two identical excavators on the same site is often close to double. The rock is the same. The driving is not.

## What the undercarriage actually is

The undercarriage is a system of parts that wear against each other: the track chain (links, pins and bushings), the sprocket that drives it, the idler at the front, the track rollers underneath and the carrier rollers on top, and the track shoes that meet the ground. When one part wears out of step with the others, it starts to wear the rest faster. That is why habits matter: they decide whether the whole system wears evenly or one part drags everything else down.

## The habits that cost the most

- Turning on one track under load. It is quick, and it scrubs the rails and the sprocket harder than anything else in the shift.
- Travelling long distances at full speed on broken ground instead of walking the machine over.
- Working across a slope for hours rather than up and down it, which loads one side of the chain continuously.
- Leaving the tracks packed at the end of a wet shift, so the material sets like concrete overnight.

## The habits that save the most

- Make gradual, two-track turns wherever there is room, and counter-rotate only when the space demands it.
- Keep travel to a minimum. Plan the dig face so the machine works from one position as long as possible, and move dump trucks to the excavator rather than the other way round.
- Alternate the turning direction through the shift, so one side of the machine is not doing all the work.
- Work up and down slopes, with the sprocket at the downhill end when travelling down.
- Use the narrowest shoe that gives enough flotation. Wide shoes on hard rock bend and crack, and they load the pins and bushings harder.
- Clean the tracks before parking, especially in clay or wet fines.

## Track tension, checked properly

Too tight is as expensive as too loose. A chain running over-tensioned wears the bushings and the sprocket teeth quickly and quietly. Check sag with the machine on level ground, at the interval in the manual, not when someone notices a problem.

The method takes five minutes. Clean the packed material out first. Move the machine forward a short distance and stop, so the slack gathers on top, then measure the sag between the carrier roller and the idler and compare it with the figure in the operator's manual. In sticky or wet material, run the tracks slightly slacker than on dry, hard ground, because packed material tightens the chain as the machine works.

## Reading the wear before it costs you

You do not need special tools to spot the early signs:

- Sprocket teeth that have turned from a rounded profile to a hooked or pointed shape.
- Shiny, scalloped wear on one side of the links, which usually points to work across a slope or a misaligned roller.
- Rollers that have stopped turning or are weeping oil.
- Track shoes with cracks running from the bolt holes.

Our technicians can measure link height, bushing wear and roller diameter against the manufacturer's wear limits and tell you how much life is left. That turns undercarriage replacement from a surprise into a planned job you can budget for.
