BODY STARTS HERE

Every year the same pattern shows up in our service logs. Breakdown calls climb through July and peak in August, and most of them trace back to something that could have been caught on a dry morning in June.

None of this is complicated work. It is the kind of check an operator can do with the machine parked, before the site turns to mud and a recovery truck becomes its own project.

## The six that matter

- Undercarriage: clear the packed soil, then measure track sag. Wet ground grinds down loose tracks faster than anything else on the machine.
- Air intake and pre-cleaner: a filter that is merely dusty in May is a blocked filter after a week of humidity.
- Cab seals and door drains: water sitting in the floor pan finds the wiring loom eventually.
- Battery terminals: clean and grease them. Damp plus corrosion is the most common no-start call we get.
- Grease points: go around the whole machine, not just the ones you can reach standing up.
- Hydraulic hose routing: look for rub marks. A hose that has been chafing quietly all summer will fail when the oil is hottest.

## How to do each check properly

The list is short on purpose, but each item is only worth doing if it is done properly. Here is what that looks like on site.

**Undercarriage.** Park on level, firm ground. Run the machine forward and back a short distance so the slack collects on the top of the track, then measure the sag at the midpoint between the carrier roller and the idler. Compare it with the figure in your operator's manual, because it differs by model. Before you measure, dig out the packed material around the sprocket and idler; a track full of dried clay reads tighter than it really is. While you are down there, look for oil weeping from the rollers and idlers. A leaking roller is cheap in June and a seized one is expensive in August.

**Air intake.** Check the pre-cleaner bowl and the restriction indicator if your machine has one. Do not tap a filter clean against the track and refit it: that cracks the media and lets dust straight into the engine. If the element is due, replace it, and keep a spare outer element in the cab for the season.

**Cab seals and drains.** Pour a cup of water at the base of the door and watch where it goes. If the drain holes are blocked with dust and grass, clear them. Lift the floor mat and look for rust or standing water, which usually means a seal has given up.

**Battery.** Disconnect, clean both terminals and clamps back to bright metal, refit them tight, and coat with terminal grease. Check that the hold-down clamp is firm, because a battery that shifts on rough ground chafes its own cables.

**Grease.** Work from the bucket back to the boom foot, then the swing bearing and the track adjusters. Pump until fresh grease shows at the joint. On a machine working in mud and water, daily greasing of the bucket and linkage pins is not overkill.

**Hoses.** Run your hand (with gloves) along the hoses at the boom and arm where they bend and cross. Shiny rub marks, cracked outer cover or a clamp that has slipped are all signs to fix now. Add a protective sleeve where two hoses touch.

## The daily habits for the wet months

The pre-season check gets the machine ready. These habits keep it that way until October.

- Clean the tracks at the end of every wet shift. Material left in overnight sets hard and loads the whole chain the next morning.
- Park on the highest, firmest ground available, with the bucket down, never in a hollow where water collects.
- Check the fuel water separator every morning. Monsoon fuel storage on site often picks up water, and a few seconds draining the separator protects the injection system.
- Keep the engine compartment and radiator free of mud and leaves, because a blocked cooler runs hot even on cool days.
- Walk the machine across soft ground rather than spinning on it. A machine that sinks is a recovery job, and a recovery job in rain is slow.

## When to stop and call us

Some symptoms are worth a call rather than another day of work:

- The engine is hard to start on damp mornings, or the warning lights flicker.
- Hydraulic functions slow down as the oil warms up.
- There is water in the engine oil (milky colour on the dipstick) or in the hydraulic tank sight glass.
- A track keeps losing tension within days of being adjusted.

Each of these is a small job when it is caught early and a large one when it is not.

## Why it pays

A machine down in the monsoon is not down for a day. Getting a technician to a hill site in heavy rain takes longer, parts move slower, and the project schedule has no slack left in it by then.

Genuine filters, seals and hoses are stocked at our central warehouse and in our branch stores, so a pre-monsoon service does not mean waiting for parts. Our technicians can typically reach a site within 24 to 48 hours from any of our ten branches.
