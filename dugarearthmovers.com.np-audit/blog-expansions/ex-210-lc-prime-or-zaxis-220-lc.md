This is the question we field most often, and the honest answer is that it depends less on the spec sheet than on what the machine will be doing for the next six years.

Both sit around 21 tonnes. Both handle road work, foundation digging and general earthmoving without complaint. The difference shows up in the duty cycle.

## Where the EX 210 LC Prime fits

175 HP, a 420 litre tank, and a 4,500 hour hydraulic oil interval. It is the straightforward choice for contractors running mixed work — a stretch of road here, a foundation there — where the machine moves between sites and the hours accumulate steadily rather than continuously.

The Prime series also brings a reinforced boom and arm and the LC Prime undercarriage, so it is built to take the knocks of being loaded, moved and set up on a new site every few weeks. For a contractor buying a first 20-tonne machine to cover a wide range of jobs, that flexibility is usually worth more than an extra few percent of digging force.

## Where the ZAXIS 220 LC fits

168 HP on paper, so slightly less than the EX on the engine line, but the numbers that matter are elsewhere: 158 kN of bucket digging force, 139 kN arm crowd, and a 5,000 hour hydraulic interval. It is built for the machine that works a full shift every day on the same job.

On a hydropower or large infrastructure contract where the excavator is the critical path, the longer service interval and the heavier digging forces earn their money back in uptime.

The ZAXIS also carries features aimed at that kind of continuous work. Its ECO mode can cut fuel consumption by up to 10% compared with PWR mode, according to Tata Hitachi. Auto Power Lift adds 6% lifting force when handling pipes or blocks, and Enhanced Power Boost adds 5% digging force for hard ground. ConSite telematics reports the machine's working status remotely, so the fleet manager knows how hard it is running without visiting the site.

## Running costs, not just purchase price

The purchase price is the number everyone compares, but over six years it is rarely the biggest one. Before choosing, it is worth putting rough figures against these:

- Fuel: hours per year multiplied by consumption in your typical work mode. Small percentage differences become large sums on a machine running full shifts.
- Servicing: how many oil and filter changes the machine needs over your planned hours, and the downtime each one costs.
- Undercarriage: on rocky or abrasive ground this is often the largest single running cost. See our note on undercarriage wear.
- Downtime: what one lost day costs your project, and how quickly parts and a technician can reach your site.
- Resale: how the machine's hours, condition and service history will look to a buyer in five or six years.

A machine that costs a little more to buy but stays on the job more days can easily be the cheaper one by the end of the contract.

## Attachments and versatility

If the machine will spend time breaking rock or trenching for utilities, check the attachment set-up before you buy. The ZAXIS 220 LC comes with a factory-fitted rock breaker piping kit and works with a rock breaker, auger, orange peel grab or quick coupler. Ask us what is fitted on the EX 210 LC Prime you are considering, and what it would take to add the attachments your work needs.

## What we actually ask buyers

- How many hours a year, honestly?
- Is it one long project or many short ones?
- Who maintains it — your own team, or us?
- What is the resale plan in five years?

The answers usually make the choice obvious.
